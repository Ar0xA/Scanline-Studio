Every mode from the original

All 43 SSTV modes from YONIQ are ported — checked mode by mode against YONIQ's own encoder and decoder, not just against each other, so a picture sent from Scanline Studio reads correctly on any other MMSSTV-family station, and vice versa.

Talks to any rig Hamlib does, not just six

YONIQ hand-coded control for a fixed list of six rig families — Yaesu, Icom CI-V, Kenwood, Ten-Tec, and JRC — and could never talk to anything outside that list. Scanline Studio never bundles a copy of Hamlib: it links against whatever Hamlib your own system already has installed, or talks to an already-running Hamlib daemon over rigctld — either way, you get Hamlib's full rig list without carrying a private fork of it that can fall out of date.

  • Linked Hamlib — in-process, against your own installed libhamlib — the widest rig list available, with no per-radio code to maintain.
  • rigctld — talks to an already-running Hamlib daemon, local or over the network, so more than one program can share the same rig, and it works on platforms without a linked build.
  • flrig and OmniRig (Windows) for stations already running them.
  • Frequency and PTT, fully async, so radio polling never blocks the decoder.

A real template editor, not a picture-plus-caption box

MMSSTV let you paste a callsign onto a picture. Scanline Studio's TX Template Editor builds full QSL-style layouts and reuses them instantly, mid-contact.

  • Text, box, image, and line elements, each independently styled, stacked, and positioned on the canvas.
  • Macros drop in your own callsign, grid, and live frequency/mode automatically; the rest of the QSO fills in as you type it.
  • Ready Rack, a 9-slot pinned strip for instant recall, backed by a full searchable Template Library in grid or list view.
  • Imports your existing YONIQ .mtm templates, so past template work carries over.
  • Perspective transform and grow-to-fill text, for layouts MMSSTV's flat paste-on couldn't do.

Every picture you've received, searchable

YONIQ kept a proprietary binary history file. The Gallery tab is a real, searchable record of every received image, tied directly to your log.

  • Full receive history, browsable and searchable, stored in a standard SQLite database.
  • Send to TX takes a received image straight into the Template Editor, and resolves the QSO it was linked to.
  • A Logged indicator shows at a glance whether a picture already has a log entry.
  • Re-decode this frame re-runs decoding on a saved recording, and Open audio file location gets you to the raw capture.

Already have YONIQ .mtm templates? They import directly. Already running rigctld or flrig for another program? Point Scanline Studio at the same instance.

About Scanline Studio

What another station transmits or expects over the air is never up for reinterpretation. Everything else was an engineering choice, and choices can be improved. Here's why this exists, what it's trying to be, and how it's actually built.

Vision

SSTV has been carried for decades by one lineage of software: MMSSTV, and later YONIQ. That lineage encodes a great deal of hard-won knowledge about how real signals behave on real bands. Scanline Studio's aim is to carry that knowledge forward onto every platform a ham actually uses today, without changing what goes over the air.

Linux hams have had QSSTV; Windows hams have had MMSSTV and YONIQ. Scanline Studio is the MMSSTV lineage, on every platform.

Goals

All 43 of YONIQ's modes, on the air exactly as before. MMSSTV and YONIQ are Windows-only C++Builder programs; Scanline Studio runs natively on Windows, Linux, and macOS, as equals, from one codebase. A template editor and receive history built for how SSTV is actually operated today, rig control through Hamlib instead of a hard-coded rig list, and a logbook that hands off to the tools you already run rather than trying to replace them. Free and open source, LGPL-3.0-or-later — the same license as upstream.

How it's built

Scanline Studio is developed with heavy use of AI-assisted engineering (Claude Code). Porting a couple of decades of C++ DSP and codec logic into a new C# codebase is exactly the kind of large, exacting, easy-to-get-subtly-wrong work where that help matters — and we'd rather say so plainly than pretend otherwise.

The tooling does not get to decide what the protocol is. Every ported mode is checked against the YONIQ source, not inferred from similar-looking code. An early port of the Scottie mode got the channel order wrong for exactly that reason — it inferred the transmit structure from the receive code instead of reading the real transmit function, and a round-trip test didn't catch it, because the encoder and decoder agreed with each other while both were wrong about the actual protocol. Every mode now gets checked against YONIQ's own encoder and decoder source specifically, not just cross-checked against itself.

MMSSTV's lineage goes back to 2000 — over two decades of accumulated engineering knowledge about how SSTV actually behaves on real bands, carried forward here rather than started over.

Found a mismatch?

If a mode decodes differently from MMSSTV or YONIQ, or transmits something another station can't read, that's a bug by definition, not a matter of opinion — please open an issue.
